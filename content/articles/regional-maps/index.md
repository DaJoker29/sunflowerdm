Exploration is the heart and soul of any sandbox campaign, so a good regional map it meant to pique their curiosity with intriguing points of interest worth exploring.

**Points of interests** can be anything that players may want to investigate. Small towns, forbidden groves, abandoned strongholds, or whatever else may snag their attention. Some are megadungeons which must be cleared in order to fulfill their quest, while others minor locations the characters pass by on the way to their objective.

As a general rule, I try to have **at least one odd or interesting detail for each location on the map.** Doesn't need to be much. For example, a monk of the sun god is seen praying at a tree with an innate warm glow. Just something to add a little color and distinction to the make every location feel alive.

These elements become landmarks which change and evolve over time. The second time the party passes that tree, guards from the nearby city have blocked off access to the tree because worship of the sun god is forbidden.

## Making Regional Maps

Many of the tools/methods mentioned in the previous article work just as well here (Inkarnate, Photoshop, Illustrator, hand-drawn), however there's a couple more which I'll add to the list for this scale.

- [Worldographer (Formerly Hexographer 2)](https://worldographer.com/) — The first games in Pavv were more of a hexcrawl to explore the island. This is the tool I used for that.
- [Wonderdraft](https://wonderdraft.net/) — While I've never used it, I have seen many taut it's performance and output. And the lack of a subscription is pretty attractive.
- [Watabou's Procgen Arcana Realm Generator](https://watabou.github.io/) — Behind Inkarnate, this is my second most frequently used map-making tool, hands down. The visual quality and speed of random generation make this great for smaller sections of your world which don't need to fit a particularly layout.

{{<callout title="Hex Grids" >}}

I usually keep two version of regional maps. One for DMs with hexes and labels to reference locations by name and measure distances, and one for the players without. I do this for two reasons:

- **Immersion.** I find when you present a hex map to players, the creative, imaginative side tends to give way to more analytical thought patterns and D&D becomes a board game instead of a roleplaying game. So I prefer to hide the mathematical minutiae behind the scenes so they can stay immersed.

- **Ownership.** I encourage my players to mark, label, and take notes all over the players' map. It makes them feel like real adventurers making a plan, rather than just following some pristine path laid out before them.

{{</callout>}}

There is a lot of flexibility here so try out different options and see what works best for you and your table.
