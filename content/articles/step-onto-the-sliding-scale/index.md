## Overworld/Continental Map

This is the key map for the campaigns I run. The continent of Pavv.

This setting only has one continent, so this is all I need. In a setting with multiple continents, I'd have an overworld map to show the world, at large, and how all the continents relate geographically to one another. Then a continental map (like this one) for each.

I say it is the key map because it is usually a starting point. It is a great place for everyone to find their bearings and start to plan their next move. Why? **Because every place that exists in the game exists on that map.** I use a small token to track the party's location and measure distance.

Now, it should be noted that I typically sandbox/open-world campaigns where players can choose where they wish to go and what objectives they wish to pursue. This is my favorite way of playing D&D. So I design my maps with that in mind. Hence, with planning and orientation being the main purpose behind this map, I strive to make a few things clear when building my maps.

**Cities & Roads.** Cities in my world are havens of (relative) safety. The wilds are known to hold countless dangers, so knowing what pathways connect them give the characters opportunity to prepare for the threats ahead.

**Regions/Biomes.** It's useful to use names and details to convey what type of enviroment each region represents. At a glance, players can intuit that the White Sand Wilds is a desert and they begin to plan accordingly.

**Alternate Routes.** Let's say the players wish to go from Solara to East Naganis. The standard route is a weeks long trek through the Deadlands, the Trickster's Green, and partways through the Pale Moon Woods. Or, they could scale the mountains between and cut a straight line through the woods. These choices bring life and shape to a campaign.

{{< callout title="Quick Note about Worldbuilding and Map-Making" >}}

Something I've neglected to mention so far is worldbuilding. That is its own topic to be explored at another time. But note this: worldbuilding and map-making are Yin and Yang, PB & J, two sides of the same coin. You can't have one without the other.

Like Tolkien, I often find it easier to start with a map and figure out what actually exists there later on. The terrain, textures, and details create a unique canvas which can influence or inspire worldbuilding.

But sometimes the reverse is true and you find the map being directly affected by the cultures, history, and lore being built. As a general rule of thumb, the lower the scale of the map, the more necessary fleshed-out worldbuilding becomes.

It's perfectly fine at the Overworld/Continental level to say that region over there is a bunch of elven kingdoms known as the Elf Lands. But as your focus zooms in to regional and city maps, you should hopefully have some idea of the names of those kingdoms, their borders, their resources, and their relationships.

Now, having said that, I find myself still going back and forth between worldbuilding and map-making regardless of how much planning ahead I do so don't fret too much about it. It's a part of the process.

{{</callout>}}

I find these maps are ever-evolving. I've lost track of how many iterations on this one over the years. I'm forever refining this map because it's the one most frequently seen. And, to be honest, it's just fun watching the world you've built take shape over time.
