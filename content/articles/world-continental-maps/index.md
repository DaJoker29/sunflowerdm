This is the key map for my campaigns: the continent of Pavv.

This setting has one continent, so this is all I need. In a setting with multiple continents, I'd have an overworld map to show the world at large, and how all the continents relate geographically to one another. Then a continental maps for each continent.

I say it is the key map because these maps are starting points. It is a great place for everyone to find their bearings and start to plan their next move. Why? **Because every place that exists in the game exists on that map.** I use a small token to track the party's location and measure distance.

Now, it should be noted that I typically sandbox/open-world campaigns where players can choose where they wish to go and what objectives they wish to pursue. This is my favorite way of running D&D, so I design my maps with that in mind. Hence, with planning and orientation being the purposes behind them, I strive to make a few things clear when building my maps.

- **Cities & Roads.** Cities in my world are havens of (relative) safety. The wilds are known to hold countless dangers, so knowing what pathways connect them give the characters opportunity to prepare for the threats ahead when planning travel.

- **Regions/Biomes.** It's useful to use names and details to convey what type of enviroment each region represents. At a glance, players can intuit that the White Sand Wilds are a desert and they plan accordingly.

- **Alternate Routes.** Let's say the players wish to go from Solara to East Naganis. The standard route is a weeks long trek through the Deadlands, the Trickster's Green, and partways through the Pale Moon Woods. Or, they could scale the mountains between and cut a straight line through the woods. These choices bring life and shape to a campaign.

{{< callout title="Quick Note about Worldbuilding and Map-Making" >}}

Something I've neglected to mention so far is worldbuilding. That is its own topic to be explored at another time, but note this: worldbuilding and map-making are Yin and Yang, PB & J, two sides of the same coin. You can't have one without the other.

Like Tolkien, I often find it easier to start with a map and figure out what actually exists there later on. The terrain, textures, and details create a unique canvas which can influence or inspire worldbuilding.

But sometimes the reverse is true and you find the map being directly affected by the cultures, history, and lore being built. As a general rule of thumb, the lower the scale of the map, the more necessary fleshed-out worldbuilding becomes.

It's perfectly fine at the Overworld/Continental level to say that region over there is a bunch of elven kingdoms known as the Elf Lands. But as your focus zooms in to regional and city maps, you should hopefully have some idea of the names of those kingdoms, their borders, their resources, and their relationships.

Now, having said that, I find myself still going back and forth between worldbuilding and map-making regardless of how much planning ahead I do so don't fret too much about it. It's a part of the process.

{{</callout>}}

## Making World & Continental Maps

{{< figure src="old-pavv-map.jpg" alt="Old map of Pavv" caption="An early iteration of Pavv's map." >}}
{{< figure src="handdrawn-map.jpg" alt="Handdrawn map" caption="My first world map, built with beans.">}}

The map of Pavv was created in [Inkarnate](https://inkarnate.com/), though it went through some iterations along the way. I'm forever refining this map because it's the one most frequently seen; and, quite honestly, it's just fun watching the world you've built take shape over time.

Now, Inkarnate isn't the only option. I've seen people use other softeware like **Adobe Illustrator** or **Photoshop**. But a cheap and easy alternative is **hand-drawing maps** and using **the beans method**.

{{<callout title="The Beans Method">}}

1. Get some paper and a bag of uncooked beans.
2. Scatter a handful of said beans onto said paper.
3. Scoot the beans around with your fingertips to create interesting shapes.
4. Trace around these shapes with a pen or pencil.
5. These shapes become the outlines for your landmasses.

Simple and effective. _Just remember to wash the beans if you decide to cook them later._

{{</callout>}}

Now, it is time for me to come clean. For years, I've said there is only one continent in my setting but that was actually a lie. While I tend to focus strictly on Pavv, it was originally a part of a much larger world I created (using the beansmethods). Go figure.

While I love using Inkarnate, hand-drawn maps still hold a cherished space in my heart. Here are a couple of great channels whose tutorials were godsends when I started drawing my first maps. I'd highly recommend giving it a shot.
