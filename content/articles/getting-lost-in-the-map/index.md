In 1954, a writer wrote to J.R.R. Tolkien seeking help with a troublesome manuscript. In his response, the father of Middle-Earth offered what I feel is one of the most impactful maxims of worldbuilding.

> "I wisely started with a map, and made the story fit (generally with meticulous care for distances). The other way about lands one in confusions and impossibilities, and in any case it is weary work to compose a map from a story—as I fear you have found."
>
> — J.R.R. Tolkien

When presenting a fictional world, as every game master or fiction author does, the audience—your players—need context. They crave it, they yearn for it, and without it they are lost at sea. This is especially important in TTRPGs because players need context for their characters to make decisions and take action. **Maps provide context.**

Imagine for a moment, the party is escaping an evil boss's lair and the rogue suggests they steal a ship and sail away. The cleric looks at them with concern, asks if they've bumped their head. Why? The lair is in a landlocked desert. The "waves of sand" described by the DM on their approach was only half-heard. These types of miscommunications happen everyday in TTRPGs, but maps are one way we alleviate them.

Now, before I go any further, I want to clarify a few things:

**Not all situations require a map.** For some instances, making a map is a colossal waste of effort and time that you are better off foregoing. We'll discuss specific cases in later articles.

**Not all maps are created equal.** Different maps serve different purposes, and some are more valuable than others. Some are made to aid the players, and some are for DMs-eyes only. Some get referenced frequently, some are frequently ignored.

Finally, **not all maps need to be meticulously detailed.** Every map has a purpose and thus requires just enough detail to serve its particular purpose.
