### Regional Maps

I wanted exploration to play a key role in campaigns I run in Pavv. So you may notice the overworld is comprised of a handful of large cities with huge swatches of wilderness in between. There are a few major roads connecting most settlements but as many players can probably assume, if you want to find anything valuable, you must trek out into the wilds.

The purpose of these maps is to show specific points of interest and use those to help orient the players geographically.

Points of interest can be anything the players may want to investigate. Small towns, forbidden groves, abandoned strongholds, or whatever. Some are full dungeons which must be explored as a part of some quest and others are just minor locations which add a bit of character to the setting.

As a general rule, however, I try to have at least one odd or interesting thing happening each location the players visit. Doesn't need to be much—a mystical tree which sings haunting tunes when the wind blows, for example. Just something to add a little color and distinction to the smaller locations to make them feel more alive. And sometimes these elements can grow more significant over time.

Orienting the players is another benefit of the regional map. I find it helpful on both the world and regional maps to use a "Party" marker to show where the characters are located at any time. It helps ground the players into the surrounding and helps them feel more engaged with the map and the setting.

    Hex Grids

    I usually have two versions of each Regional Map. One with hexes (for me), and one without (for the players). In my experience, when players look at a hex map the roleplaying and imagination of D&D becomes replaced with analytical, geometrical thought patterns. In other words, it breaks immersion, so I prefer to handle the mathematical minutiae behind the scenes so the players can stay in the moment with their characters.

### City Maps

In the wilds of Pavv, the cities are the closest thing to a safe haven the characters will find. Cities serve as regional hubs they can return to between adventures for the typical services and amenities—food, rest, equipment and upgrades.

But, at the same time, each city is its own tapestry of culture, politics, and history waiting to be explored. For this reason, I don't even start building a city map until I have at least a solid amount of worldbuilding in place for the people who live there so I can, at the very least, address some of those things in the cities layout and design.

Population and demographics are one of the first things I try to answer when building a city map. This will usually be determined by the location's history. Who first settled this land? What type of culture did they bring? Has their culture grown? What was their relationship to other cultures?

    Layering History

    One of my favorite tools for building a place's history is what I call layering. Inspired by Matt Colville, this technique is simply stacking different cultures on top of one another through time and seeing what comes out on the other side.

    For example, let's say a group of introverted wizards founded a small hamlet to practice their arcane rituals in peace, away from the bustling metropolis. Then, dwarven lumberjacks began migrating there to harvest magical timber from a nearby grove. Centuries later, the city of Mystic Grove is world-renowned for producing the finest wands, staves, and spell paper on the market. Tourists flock to see the enchanted tools levitating at the Sorcerer's Mill. The kegs and taps at the local tavern automagically replenish from the ensorceled barrels.

    Doesn't that sound way cooler than "a dwarven city" or a "a town of mages".

### Town Maps

Pointless. I don't use them. Make a list of shops and a few NPCs, if you want.

### Dungeon Maps

In the past I've avoided running dungeons because I find just the thought of them claustrophobic and unsettling. However, sometimes that's exactly what you want and in those moments there is no better solution than a dungeon. And when done properly, they can provide a fantastic experience that'll leave your players traumatized for years.

Dungeons in my games are meant to be spooky experiences. To accommodate this, I try to use theater of the mind as much as possible when running them. With the right words and subtle descriptions, the player will build a much more convincing hellscape in their mind's eye than you can on paper or screen.

The maps I do use are purely for tracking progress and guiding my narration. I use them in conjunction with a detailed key to let me know what each room contains. The contents of the room and the entrances/exits are the key elements needed for running dungeons.

Where as regional maps require a good amount of worldbuilding, these require a good amount of imagination. The devil is in the details: most of which you aren't going to be able to put on a map. So having a secondary document to track those details is vital.
