Exploration is the heart and soul of any sandbox campaign. A good regional map offer a menu of curiosities for the players to explore. It should be filled with intriguing points of interest.

**Points of interests** can be anything that players may want to investigate. Small towns, forbidden groves, abandoned strongholds, or whatever else may snag the player's attention. Sometimes they are full megadungeons which must be cleared for the quest, and sometimes they are just minor locations the characters pass by on the way to their objective.

As a general rule, however, try to **have at least one odd or interesting situation for each location on the map.** Doesn't need to be much—a monk of the sun god praying at a tree with an innate warm glow. Just something to add a little color and distinction to the make every location feel alive.

And sometimes these elements grow more significant over time. As characters move across the region, this locations become landmarks that can change and evolve over time. For example, the second time my characters passed that tree, guards from the nearby city had blocked off access to the tree because worship of the sun god was forbidden there.

{{<callout title="Hex Grids" >}}

I usually keep two version of regional maps. One for DMs with hexes and labels to reference locations by name and measure distances, and one for the players without. I do this for two reasons:

- **Immersion.** I find when you present a hex map to players, the creative, imaginative side tends to give way to more analytical thought patterns and D&D becomes a board game instead of a roleplaying game. So I prefer to hide the mathematical minutiae behind the scenes so they can stay immersed.

- **Ownership.** I encourage my players to mark, label, and take notes all over the players' map. It makes them feel like real adventurers making a plan, rather than just following some pristine path laid out before them.
