In 1954, a writer wrote to J.R.R. Tolkien seeking help with a troublesome manuscript. In his response, the father of Middle-Earth offered a good maxim for writers, worldbuilders, and gamemasters alike.

> "I wisely started with a map, and made the story fit (generally with meticulous care for distances). The other way about lands one in confusions and impossibilities, and in any case it is weary work to compose a map from a story—as I fear you have found."
>
> — J.R.R. Tolkien

As with all storytellers, your job as a gamemaster is to present a fictional world that feels real to your players. And to do so, the **players need context.** They crave it, they yearn for it, and without it they float aimlessly through the narrative. Context is especially important in TTRPGs because players need proper context to make decisions. **Maps provide context.**

Imagine, a party is escaping an evil boss's castle lair. The Rogue suggests they steal a ship and sail away. The Cleric looks at them with concern, asking did they hit their head. Why? The lair is in a landlocked desert. Earlier, the DM described dunes appearing like "waves of sand" and wires got crossed. These types of miscommunications happen everyday in TTRPGs, but maps are one way we alleviate them.

Now, before we get started, I want to clarify a few things:

- **Not all situations require a map.** For some instances, making a map is a colossal waste of effort and time that you are better off foregoing. We'll discuss specific cases in later articles.

- **Not all maps are created equal.** Different maps serve different purposes, and some are more valuable than others. Some are made to aid the players, and some are for DMs-eyes only. Some get referenced frequently, whiles others are frequently ignored.

- **Not all maps need to be meticulously detailed.** Every map has a purpose and thus requires just enough detail to serve its particular purpose.
